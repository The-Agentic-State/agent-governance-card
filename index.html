The Agent Governance Card

State governments are moving past chatbots into agentic AI — software that takes actions: it uses tools, touches data, sends things, files things, completes multi-step tasks with real autonomy. The governance machinery states rely on today — intake, security review, procurement, audit — was not built for software that acts. What's missing is not another framework; it's a simple, per-deployment record of the questions every state has to be able to answer before an agent goes live: what is it allowed to do, what does it touch, who answers for it when it acts, how does a person step in — and what does it cost to leave.

The Agent Governance Card is that record. A product team completes it before an agentic system gets production access to state data, tools, or users. It is a snapshot of what is true at go-live, not a list of promises; unknown and not in place are formal answers, because a missing mechanism recorded plainly is a governance finding, not a failure. From the team's own answers the card computes an Impact Level that routes proportionate obligations — and when several states fill the same card, their deployments become comparable.

We do it with two instruments:

Instrument 1 · the record

The schema

One canonical, machine-readable file is the source of truth: the explicit metadata a state needs to know about every agent — 45 plain-language questions in 12 dimensions, a 9-field controlled spine for cross-state comparability, an explicit public layer (what citizens get to see), and the derivation rules that compute the Impact Level. Built on OMB M-25-21/22, UK ATRS, NIST, and the agentic standards work — and stress-tested against 20 real deployments before we ask anyone to trust it.

Instrument 2 · the way in

The skill

Nobody fills a 45-field form for fun. The skill is an AI interview that navigates the schema for you: plain-language questions one at a time, advice on what each option means in your context, honest handling of unknowns — then it computes your Impact Level and emits the filled card, human-readable and machine-readable. A governance thinking partner, pointed at producing the record.

A voluntary instrument. The card records, computes, and recommends — it never blocks. Its recommendations become gates only where an adopting authority (a state CIO or agency directive) makes a completed card a precondition for production access.

The schema — card v0.3. This is the actual canonical file (YAML, versioned in GitHub), dimension by dimension. Every face of the card — the skill, a form, a document — renders from it. Questions are the content layer, which is editable.

The Impact Level is computed, not chosen. Two independent legs; the higher wins.
Leg 1 · OMB high-impact test high impact

From C7 alone. The chatbot gave official-sounding guidance on housing, employment and consumer-protection rules that users could reasonably treat as a principal basis for decisions with legal effects.

Leg 2 · Operational tier low

From C8/C10/C11/C11a/C12. It only informs — no tools, no actions, no personal data of record, no red-line trifecta. On its own this would have been a Low.

Final high

The higher of the two. Part 2 (Extended) is required, plus the full OMB minimum-practice set for high-impact systems.

What filling the card surfaced

None of these are hidden facts — each traces to a public document. What the card does is make their absence a required field rather than something nobody thought to ask. Note that most of them are recorded as an ordinary No, not as a blank: only two fields here are “not in place.” A card that looks complete can still be telling you the safeguards are missing — which is why the level is computed from the answers rather than from how full the form looks.

C7aNo determination record

Nobody wrote down who judged this system's risk, in what role, or on what basis. The most consequential judgement about the system was never recorded.

E13No appeal route

A user harmed by relying on a wrong answer had nowhere to go. The only channel was a feedback form.

E9No live monitoring

No documented monitoring of answer quality in production — the errors were found by journalists, not by the city.

C20No assessable pre-deployment testing

OTI claimed red-teaming and 95–99% accuracy; the only artifact was a one-page summary the Comptroller's auditors found too thin to assess.

E18No exit plan

No documented plan for shutdown, data portability, or continuity — for a system that ran for over two years.

C22 · E15Disclaimers as the only safeguard

Fully automated legal guidance, protected by a disclaimer the bot itself contradicted when it told a user it could be relied on for professional advice.

E5No named accountable human

Accountability sat at office level. No individual output could be traced to a responsible person.
